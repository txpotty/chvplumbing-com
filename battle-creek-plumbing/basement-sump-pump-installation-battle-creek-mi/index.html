    Plan on 4 to 8 hours for a standard install. Digging a new pit and patching the slab can take the whole day, and tricky electrical access or a long discharge run through a finished wall adds time on top of that. You get a time estimate first.

    Watch for constant running, rapid cycling or grinding noises. A sticking float or rust streaks on the housing also mean trouble, as is water sitting above the switch level without the pump starting. Do not wait for the storm.

    Swapping an identical plug-in pump is doable for a handy homeowner.     A primary pump does the everyday work. A secondary pump, often on a battery, waits in the same pit, and it kicks on if the primary fails, loses power, or gets overwhelmed by more water than it can move during a heavy storm. Two pumps, two check valves.

    For some homeowners, a same-model pump swap is a fair weekend job. Cutting a new pit or adding a backup is not, because those mistakes end in flooding, cracked slabs or electrical hazards, and fixing them costs more than paying for the install up front. Leave those to us.

    Discharge belongs in the yard, a dry well, or an approved drainage area at least 10 feet from the foundation. Most local codes do not allow sump water in the sanitary sewer, and it can draw a fine.
