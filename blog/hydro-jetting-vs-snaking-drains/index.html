    Hydro Jetting vs. Snaking: Which Drain Cleaning Method Do You Need?

    By Aaron Mitchell · August 22, 2026 · 6 min read

    Know the Clog First

    Not every clog is the same, and the tool should match what is actually in the pipe. A hair clog in a shower and a kitchen line coated with grease are two different problems. Clogs fall into two groups, a single plug at one spot or buildup along the whole pipe wall, and figuring out which one you have is the first thing a good tech does. It starts with a couple of questions.

    Scale is the slow version of buildup. Old cast iron drains collect scale and rust that close the opening a little every year. A 4-inch cast iron line with heavy scale might have the flow area of a 2-inch pipe, which is why it clogs again a week after a cable goes through it. Cabling cannot scrape that off.

    Well water across much of the state tends to be harder and often carries iron, which discolors sinks and clogs aerators.

    How a Drain Snake Works

    A snake, or drain auger, is a coiled steel cable spun by a hand crank or a motor. The tip carries a cutting head or a spring that bores into the clog and either breaks it up or hooks it. Hand augers handle sinks and tubs, a closet auger handles toilets, a small drum machine handles laundry and kitchen lines, and a sectional machine with a 3/4-inch cable reaches a main line out to 100 feet or more. It is fast and cheap.

    Snaking is the right first call for a single hard blockage. A toilet stopped by a toy or a main plugged by roots at one joint both clear well with a cable. The weakness is that a cable opens a hole roughly the size of its cutter, so grease and scale on the rest of the pipe wall stay right where they were. A clog that returns within weeks is the giveaway.

    Cable size matters as much as the machine. A 1/4-inch hand cable is right for a bathroom sink but useless on a 4-inch main. Running too big a cable through a small trap can crack it, and running a small cable through a main just pokes a hole through a root mass that grows back within weeks. Matching the two takes experience.

    How Hydro Jetting Works

    A jetter pushes water through a hose at roughly 3,000 to 4,000 psi for residential lines. The nozzle has one forward jet to cut through the clog and several rear jets that pull the hose along and scour the walls. As the tech reels the hose back, the rear jets strip grease, scale, sludge and roots off the pipe all the way around, and the debris flushes downstream to the sewer. What is left is a nearly clean pipe wall.

    Jetting shines on greasy kitchen lines and scaled cast iron. Commercial kitchens jet their lines regularly for the same reason. It also clears sand and silt that a cable just pushes around, and a jetted line usually stays clear far longer than a cabled one, often years instead of months on a grease problem. It costs more per visit.

    Jetting needs a way in. An exterior cleanout is the best access point, since it keeps the mess out of the house. Homes without one may need a cleanout installed first, usually a quick job with a fitting and a riser at the foundation, and that new access point pays for itself the next time the line needs work. A toilet flange can serve as access when nothing else will.

    Choosing Between Them

    Jetting is not safe for every pipe. High pressure can blow through cast iron that is paper thin or clay that is already broken. That is why a good tech runs a camera first on an older line, both to see what the clog is and to check whether the pipe can take a jetter without making the problem worse. Without the camera, a cleaning can end up as an excavation.

    Here is the rough rule we use. One hard plug in pipe that is in decent shape gets snaked. A kitchen line that clogs every few months or a main coated with grease or scale gets a camera and then a jetter, and that combination usually stops the repeat calls for a long time. Cost plays into it too.

    When to Call a Professional

    Rental drum machines are where a lot of homeowner drain jobs go sideways. A cable that kinks inside the pipe can get stuck or whip around and hurt someone. If a plunger and a small hand auger have not cleared it, or more than one drain is backing up, call CHV Plumbing at (810) 380-5918 and describe which fixtures are affected, because that tells the tech which line to start on and which tools to bring. Mention when the last cleaning was.
