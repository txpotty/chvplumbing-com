    Two warranties apply. The manufacturer covers the toilet. Most brands give one to five years on parts like the fill valve and flapper and a longer term, sometimes lifetime, on the vitreous china, while our own guarantee covers any leak traced back to how we set it.

    Cracks in the tank or bowl are the clearest sign. Hairline cracks can be hard to spot. Look for a slow drip on the floor that comes back after you dry it, or frequent clogs on an early low-flow model with a narrow trapway. So is a toilet you have rebuilt twice in three years.

    Lake-sourced municipal water in Michigan is moderately hard, so scale gradually builds up in water heaters and faucets. Hard water leaves scale in the rim jets and on the fill valve over time. Flush power drops as jets clog. A toilet with a well-glazed trapway and an easy-to-reach fill valve holds up better, and a cup of vinegar left in the overflow tube overnight now and then keeps the rim jets open.

    Lower water bills come first. An old 3.5 gallon toilet uses almost three times the water of a 1.28 gallon model. Newer bowls also have wider glazed trapways that clog less, and the smoother porcelain stays cleaner between scrubbings than an old pitted bowl ever did.

    TOTO and American Standard do well in independent flush tests, and Kohler is close behind, with parts at most supply houses. For a budget, Gerber and Mansfield hold up. We match the model to your rough-in and how the bathroom gets used.
